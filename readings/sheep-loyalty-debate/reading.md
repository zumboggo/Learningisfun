# Your Dad Stole a Sheep. Now What?

Your father has stolen the neighbour’s sheep. This is inconvenient. You love your father. You also support the radical principle that sheep should remain with their owners.

In [*The Sheep Behind Our House*](https://zumboggo.github.io/Learningisfun/#/game/play/4d41acf22bed424112a67e5d7b3a79f0), Jun faces this problem. His father, Bo, once carried him home when he could not walk. Now Bo asks for silence. Shen wants her sheep back—and the market morning she lost searching for it. Gratitude has met somebody else’s grocery bill.

**Confucius makes the question harder.** In *Analects* 13.18, a ruler praises a son who reported his father for stealing a sheep. Confucius replies that uprightness can lie in fathers and sons concealing one another’s wrongdoing. He gives family protection genuine moral weight. The short passage does not tell us what happens to the owner. Jun, Bo, Shen and their attempted repair are additions in our episode, not details from the ancient text. [Read the passage](https://ctext.org/analects/zi-lu/)

The strongest family-loyalty argument is not “Dad gets a crime subscription.” It is that relationships create special duties. A parent is not interchangeable with a stranger. Protecting a loved person from public disgrace might leave room for private correction and repair. But who decides that the repair is enough—the family, or the person outside its gate?

**Now enter Joseph Henrich, carrying a very large book.** His 2020 *The WEIRDest People in the World* explores Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich and Democratic populations. “WEIRD” describes a research category, not a prize for being the most normal human. [About the book](https://heb.fas.harvard.edu/publication/weirdest-people-world-how-west-became-psychologically-peculiar-and-particularly)

Henrich argues that the medieval Western Church’s marriage rules, including restrictions on cousin marriage, helped weaken tightly connected extended-family networks. Over generations, people developed more relationships and cooperation beyond their kin. The Church could also benefit when people without heirs left it their wealth. Changing family life could strengthen an institution’s resources and influence. Convenient, if you happen to be that institution. [Research background](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2019/11/roman-catholic-church-ban-in-the-middle-ages-loosened-family-ties/)

This is a historical explanation to examine, not proof that banning cousin marriage magically deletes corruption. Henrich and colleagues found patterns connecting Church exposure, kinship and psychology; their findings do not establish a single cause for fair institutions. Confucius asks what we owe one another. Henrich asks how different habits of obligation developed. Explaining a belief’s history does not prove it right. [The study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31699908/)

Here is the awkward twist: an institution can protect its favourites too. Imagine a school hiding a teacher’s wrongdoing to protect its reputation. Has it escaped family favouritism—or invented a larger family with a logo?

**Debate motion: When family loyalty conflicts with justice for an outsider, justice should come first.**

Before choosing a side, agree what “justice” means here. Must it involve reporting Bo, or could a private agreement chosen freely by Shen qualify? Use one episode detail and explain the strongest opposing reason before answering it.

**Test your position:** If Bo returns the sheep and compensates Shen, is reporting him still necessary? If the official is cruel or corrupt, does protecting Bo become more defensible? Would your answer change if Bo were your father? If so, is that hypocrisy—or a real moral obligation?

Finally, draw your boundary: **When does loyalty mean keeping someone’s secret, and when does it mean refusing to help them become worse?** The sheep would like a decision before lunch.
