# Copywork: A Greenhouse for Originality

A teacher plans another spectacular lesson. There will be surprises, movement, probably a complicated set of instructions. Tomorrow, the whole machine must be invented again. In David’s account of his early teaching, the wish to keep everything fresh became exhausting. Students spent too much attention figuring out the activity and too little attention inside the ideas.

His change of direction was simple: dependable routines. The greenhouse is his metaphor. A greenhouse creates conditions in which something can grow; its structure serves the living thing inside it. A classroom routine can do something similar. When students understand the procedure, they have more room to take an intellectual risk. This is a teaching conviction drawn from experience, not a claim that one routine solves every classroom problem.

Copywork is one such routine. A learner reproduces a short passage accurately, slowing down enough to encounter choices that fast reading can miss. A sentence has a length. A verb carries an attitude. A comma holds a thought open; a full stop closes it. What looked effortless begins to reveal its construction.

## Practice at what?

Writing more does not automatically mean noticing more. A student may keep rehearsing the same vague claim, dropped quotation, or unexplained example. The answer is not to silence that student’s voice. It is to place useful possibilities within reach. Musicians work with compositions they did not invent. Artists study other artists’ decisions. Writers, too, can study a model before adapting what they learn.

Copying is only the beginning of the routine proposed here. The important movement is from reproduction to attention, then from attention to an independent choice. A page copied while thinking about lunch may be beautiful handwriting practice. It does not yet show that the writer understands the passage.

Consider this original example: “The clock swallowed our last minute.” Naming personification identifies a feature. Explaining that “swallowed” makes time feel like a hungry opponent identifies an effect. Trying “The inbox swallowed my quiet morning” transfers the move into a different situation. A useful explanation stays close to particular words and what they make this reader notice.

## A historical example, with the differences kept clear

Benjamin Franklin describes studying essays from *The Spectator*. He reduced sentences to brief notes, waited, rebuilt the essays in his own words, and compared his versions with the originals. He also experimented with verse and rearranging notes to practise vocabulary and order. This was reconstruction and imitation, not simply copying paragraphs by hand. His account supplies a historical example of deliberate work with models, not proof that one modern classroom routine guarantees better writing. [Franklin’s autobiography](https://dev.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/36151/pg36151-images.html)

These distinctions matter. Copywork reproduces wording. Memorization recalls it without looking. Imitation adapts a pattern or technique. A commonplace book collects passages and ideas for later use. A teacher may combine them, but should know which action students are practising and why.

## Structure that leaves room for choice

The workshop lesson uses a small cycle: copy accurately, notice specific choices, explain an effect, and borrow a move for new meaning. The model is a starting point. The eventual goal is a writer who can decide whether a long sentence builds useful momentum, whether repetition earns its place, or whether a particular metaphor clarifies anything at all.

That also means choosing models generously. Excellence does not belong to one accent, genre, culture, or century. A clear scientific explanation, a spoken-word performance, a sharply reasoned paragraph, and a carefully observed scene can each teach something different. Selection should follow the writing problem students face. Explain unfamiliar context before asking them to learn from the language.

There is a genuine tension here. A model can open possibilities, but it can also become a template that students obey mechanically. The safeguard is meaningful transfer: new subject matter, new reasoning, and a reason for borrowing this particular choice. In David’s full assignment, students extend an argument or inquiry rather than merely changing a few nouns. His description and rubric are available separately in this workshop.

The greenhouse earns its place when the learner can eventually grow beyond it. Copywork is most useful when students leave with more choices available to their own voices.
