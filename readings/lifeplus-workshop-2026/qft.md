# QFT: Give Students a Share in the Discovery

The teacher asks a thoughtful question. Three hands rise. A familiar conversation begins: the teacher knows where the lesson should go, several students guess the route, and the rest wait to find out whether they are right. The question may be excellent. The distribution of intellectual work still deserves attention.

David’s workshop begins from a different possibility: students can help decide what deserves investigation. Reading about an archaeological discovery is interesting; standing at the edge of the dig and deciding where to look next offers another kind of involvement. The comparison is a metaphor, not a promise that every classroom question will uncover something new to the world. Something can be known by scholars and still be a real discovery for the learner.

## A routine for entering the unknown

The Question Formulation Technique, developed by the Right Question Institute, gives question-making a deliberate structure. Its sequence separates generating possibilities from examining and choosing among them. That separation matters: if the first unusual question is immediately corrected or answered, the group may learn that safe questions are the only welcome ones.

A Question Focus, or QFocus, supplies a shared starting point: a statement, image, object, or other stimulus rather than a question from the teacher. Participants produce questions under four rules: generate many; withhold discussion, judgment, and answers; record the questions as spoken; and turn statements into questions. They examine open and closed questions, consider the uses and limits of both, change questions between types, and select priorities for a stated purpose. They decide what to do next and reflect on the thinking process. [RQI overview](https://media.rightquestion.org/resources/Overview-of-the-Question-Formulation-Technique.pdf)

Source: The Question Formulation Technique was created by Dan Rothstein and Luz Santana, co-founders of the Right Question Institute (rightquestion.org). See [RQI’s explanation and reuse terms](https://rightquestion.org/what-is-the-qft/).

## Ownership needs somewhere to go

Inviting questions is a beginning. What happens afterward tells students how seriously their questions are being taken. Imagine a group asking why a school rule exists, who it helps, and whether it has exceptions. If the activity ends with a count of how many questions they typed, curiosity has been displayed but not pursued. If the group chooses a question, identifies evidence, and revises an initial explanation, the question has become part of learning.

An open question is not automatically superior to a closed one. A closed question can establish an essential fact; an open one can investigate explanation or meaning. “Did the policy change?” and “Why did the policy change?” do different jobs. Students need judgment about the job, not a rule that one type always wins.

The teacher remains responsible for the purpose, usable sources, time, and conditions for participation. Student ownership does not require the teacher to pretend that every answer is equally supported. It asks the teacher to leave some real decisions for students and then help them investigate responsibly.

## Why this workshop starts with a shared text

A reading gives people something stable to revisit. A participant can reread a sentence, identify an assumption, or connect two details while someone else is still working through the opening. Written contributions let several people formulate ideas at once, rather than making every contribution wait for a single speaking turn. That does not mean typing automatically creates thoughtful discussion. The replies still need attention, evidence, and follow-through.

There is a useful workplace parallel. In his 2017 shareholder letter, Jeff Bezos described Amazon meetings beginning with silent reading of narrative six-page memos. The relevant idea here is shared preparation before discussion, not a claim that slides are always inferior or that every classroom needs six pages. [Amazon’s 2017 letter](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2017-letter-to-shareholders)

In this workshop, LIF Text Discussion is the space for experiencing student-generated inquiry. It can hold private question drafting, publication, voting, and replies as the facilitator opens the relevant stages. The complete QFT includes work the group may do aloud or on paper, including changing question types and reflecting. The software’s buttons are not a substitute for those intellectual moves.

## A routine worth carrying home

The same habit can travel beyond a class discussion. Before an essay, a student can generate possible questions, discover which need evidence, and choose one worth pursuing. Before teaching a unit, a teacher can identify what is actually uncertain rather than beginning with a list of activities.

David’s broader priorities are to ask questions, remember useful knowledge, and write well. These are workshop priorities, not a complete theory of education. Knowledge gives inquiry something to work with; questions give knowledge a direction; writing makes the developing thought available for examination. The routine is simple enough to remember: read, think, discuss, and write. What people discover within it need not be predictable.
