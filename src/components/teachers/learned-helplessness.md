## The elephant and the tiny stake

![Elephant and tiny stake](/Learningisfun/images/teacher-example-elephant.webp)

An original AI-generated illustration of a familiar metaphor. This is not a documentary photograph or evidence about how elephants behave.

Look at the difference in size. A huge elephant stands beside a tiny wooden stake, connected to it by a thin rope. Surely an animal that powerful could move it. Why might it remain where it is?

A popular motivational story offers an answer: when the elephant was small, it tried to escape and failed. Later, although it had grown stronger, it continued to expect failure. Its old experience had become a rule for its future.

It is a memorable story, but we should treat it as a parable, not an established scientific account of elephant training. Real animal behaviour is more complicated. The image is useful because it gives us a human question: **When does remembering a past limitation stop us from noticing a present possibility?**

Imagine failing three mathematics tests. At first you think, “I did badly on these topics.” Later the thought becomes, “I am bad at mathematics.” Eventually it becomes, “Nothing I do will make a difference.” Those statements sound similar, but they make very different predictions. The last one can prevent the next experiment: a new explanation, a different practice method, or a conversation with a teacher.

Last week's reading explored the courage in “Invictus.” Its captain could not command the weather, but steering still mattered. This week asks what happens when a person stops believing that the wheel is connected to anything.

## A discovery—and a correction

![Martin Seligman speaking at the University of Pennsylvania in 2009](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/1a/Martin_Seligman_Philadelphia_2009.jpg?utm_source=commons.wikimedia.org&utm_campaign=index&utm_content=original)

Psychologist Martin Seligman in 2009. Photo: D. Myles Cullen, U.S. Army/Department of Defense; public domain. [Image source](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Martin_Seligman_Philadelphia_2009.jpg).

In the 1960s, Martin Seligman, Steven Maier, and colleagues studied how animals responded to uncontrollable events. In experiments involving unpleasant electric shocks, some dogs could stop them by acting; others could not. Later, when escape was possible in a different setting, many dogs previously exposed to uncontrollable shocks did not escape. The researchers called the pattern learned helplessness: earlier uncontrollability seemed to reduce later attempts to act.

The experiments caused animal suffering. They are historical research to examine, not activities to reproduce. Nor can animal behaviour explain every human experience.

In 2016, Maier and Seligman revised their explanation using later neuroscience, largely involving rodents. Their account suggested that passivity during prolonged aversive stimulation was a default response; experiencing control helped suppress it. What was learned was control, rather than passivity itself. The familiar term remained, but the proposed mechanism changed. That correction matters: scientists can keep a useful observation while revising why they think it happens. [The researchers' reassessment](https://ppc.sas.upenn.edu/sites/default/files/learnedhelplessnessat50.pdf).

For this reading, think of the human pattern as an expectation: “My actions will not affect the outcome.” It is different from knowing that a particular action cannot work. If a door is locked, repeatedly pulling its handle may be pointless. Looking for a key, asking someone to open it, or finding another entrance could still matter.

The difficulty comes when “this handle did not work” grows into “there is no possible way through.” A conclusion that once fitted the evidence may become too broad, or remain unchanged after the situation changes.

## The New Yorker connection

Maria Konnikova's 2015 New Yorker article, “Trying to Cure Depression, but Inspiring Torture,” traces learned helplessness research and its disturbing connection to coercive interrogation. It shows how knowledge intended to understand distress can also be misused. Our classroom question goes in the opposite direction: how can environments help people regain meaningful choice? [Read the article](https://www.newyorker.com/science/maria-konnikova/theory-psychology-justified-torture).

That question changes how we judge people. A quiet student might seem uninterested. But perhaps earlier attempts to contribute were ignored. A person who does not ask for help might appear stubborn. Perhaps previous requests brought embarrassment instead of assistance. These are possible explanations, not conclusions we can draw by looking at someone.

It also changes how we offer encouragement. “You can do anything!” is difficult to believe, because it is untrue. “Here is one thing you can try, and I will help you see what happens” offers something more concrete.

## Story one: a word at a water pump

![Helen Keller seated beside her teacher Anne Sullivan in 1898](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/f5/Helenkellerannesullivan1898.jpg?utm_source=commons.wikimedia.org&utm_campaign=index&utm_content=original)

Helen Keller, left, with Anne Sullivan in 1898. Photograph attributed to Alexander Graham Bell; public domain. [Image source](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Helenkellerannesullivan1898.jpg).

Helen Keller lost her sight and hearing after an illness when she was nineteen months old. Her situation was not a tiny, imaginary obstacle. She needed a way to communicate that did not depend on seeing or hearing. Anne Sullivan became her teacher in 1887, when Keller was six. [American Foundation for the Blind chronology](https://www.afb.org/about-afb/history/helen-keller/biography-and-chronology/chronology).

In her autobiography, Keller describes Sullivan spelling words into her hand. Keller could copy the movements before she understood what they meant. The fingers moved; the meaning remained out of reach. Learning the difference between the words for a mug and water was especially frustrating. During one difficult lesson she broke a doll.

Then Sullivan took her outside to a water pump. As water flowed over one of Keller's hands, Sullivan spelled “water” into the other. Suddenly the pattern of movements and the cool stream belonged together. The movements were not just a game. They could name something in the world.

Keller recalled wanting to know the names of objects around her. She returned to the broken doll and felt regret. The account does not end with everything becoming easy. Instead, she writes:

> “There were barriers still, it is true, but barriers that could in time be swept away.”

[Helen Keller, The Story of My Life, chapter IV](https://www.afb.org/online-library/story-my-life/part-1/chapter-iv), published in 1903; public-domain text.

We should not diagnose Keller with learned helplessness from a story. Her experience illustrates a related point: what appears to be inability can change when a person gains access to a usable method. Sullivan did more than encourage Keller to believe. She offered patient teaching through touch, connected to a real experience.

Try a thought experiment. Suppose someone gives you instructions in an alphabet you cannot read, then calls you lazy when you cannot follow them. Repeating the same instructions more loudly does not create access. A translation might. A demonstration might. A teacher who notices the problem might.

Keller's story challenges the idea that independence means needing nobody. Another person's knowledge helped her exercise more agency—more ability to act and communicate. Support and independence can grow together.

For a student today, the equivalent is usually less dramatic: a diagram instead of another paragraph, a worked example, extra processing time, or a chance to practise privately. The useful question is not only “How hard am I trying?” It is also “What would make this task understandable and accessible?”

## Story two: a windmill from discarded parts

![One of William Kamkwamba's early windmills built from salvaged materials](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/f0/William_Kamkwambas_old_windmill.jpg?utm_source=commons.wikimedia.org&utm_campaign=index&utm_content=original)

One of William Kamkwamba's early windmills in Malawi. Photo credited to Tom Rielly, shared by Erik Hersman, via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:William_Kamkwambas_old_windmill.jpg); [CC BY 2.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/2.0/). No additional edits.

At fourteen, William Kamkwamba was living in Malawi amid poverty and famine. He built an electricity-generating windmill using spare parts and information from a library book. It brought electricity to his family's home. The achievement later brought him to the TED stage. [Kamkwamba's 2007 talk](https://www.ted.com/talks/william_kamkwamba_how_i_built_a_windmill) and [2009 talk](https://www.ted.com/talks/william_kamkwamba_how_i_harnessed_the_wind).

In a TED interview, he recalled people thinking he was crazy while he searched through rubbish for materials. A picture in a book helped him persist: it showed that the machine was possible. As he explained:

> “Somewhere, someone did this thing. If somebody did this thing, I can also do it.”

His experiments were not all successful. An attempt to produce biogas using his mother's cooking pot failed; he had tried to hurry a process that needed time. He described learning patience and looking for better ways to do things.

He also discussed being bullied at school. When older pupils demanded money, he was initially afraid to tell anyone. As he grew older, he learned to seek help. This was a different kind of change: recognising that another person's support could be part of his response. [TED interview with Kamkwamba](https://blog.ted.com/how_my_windmill/).

A windmill is easy to turn into a simple hero story: one determined boy defeats every obstacle. But that would lose the most useful details. A book supplied information. Existing machinery supplied a model. Discarded objects supplied materials. Other people could supply protection or support. His actions worked through resources and relationships.

Notice, too, that his confidence had a reason behind it. He could point to evidence that someone had built a windmill. He still had to learn whether his own design would work. That is different from guaranteeing success before beginning.

The failed experiment matters as much as the successful invention. If failure means “I am incapable,” it closes the investigation. If it means “This method did not work under these conditions,” there are questions left to ask. What information was missing? What needed more time? Who understands this process better?

Kamkwamba's life is not evidence that poverty disappears when someone thinks positively, or that people facing poverty are responsible for their circumstances. His story offers a narrower, more useful possibility: even within severe limits, a specific action may open a possibility that was previously unavailable. We are using his account as an example of resourcefulness and seeking help, not claiming he had a psychological diagnosis.

## Story three: learning what failure means

In a small 1975 study, psychologist Carol Dweck worked with twelve children aged eight to thirteen who reacted strongly to failure. One training approach emphasised successful performance. Another helped children connect setbacks with effort rather than a fixed lack of ability.

When failure occurred afterwards, the children given success-focused training still showed declining performance. Those in the attribution-retraining condition maintained or improved their performance. An attribution is an explanation for why something happened. The study suggested that practising success alone might not teach someone how to handle a setback. [Dweck's original study](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0077149).

Twelve children is a very small sample, and one older experiment does not establish a universal solution. It also does not justify blaming every failure on insufficient effort. A useful classroom interpretation is to investigate changeable causes: preparation, strategy, feedback, time, and access to help. Sometimes the task or environment needs to change, too.

## Where the pattern can hide

The following are invented everyday examples, not diagnoses of real students.

**The blank mathematics page.** A student immediately says, “I cannot do this.” The teacher discovers that the difficulty starts with fractions, several steps before the current topic. The next useful action is targeted practice with that missing skill. Repeating the hardest question twenty times would provide twenty more failures, not twenty useful lessons.

**The silent group member.** A student has had ideas dismissed and now contributes nothing. A fairer group gives each person time to propose one approach before deciding. Here, changing the environment matters. The student should not have to develop extraordinary confidence just to receive ordinary respect.

**The language learner.** After classmates laugh at a pronunciation mistake, a student stops speaking. A small next experiment might be rehearsing one sentence with a supportive partner. The class also needs to stop ridiculing mistakes. Courage is easier to practise where others make it safe to learn.

**The person who always hands over the device.** “I am useless with technology,” they say, before trying. Someone can help by guiding one step and letting them perform it, rather than taking over the entire task. A successful action becomes evidence: “I did that part myself.”

These situations can resemble helplessness, but similar behaviour may have other explanations, including tiredness, confusion, anxiety, or a reasonable decision that a task is not worthwhile. A label should invite curiosity, never replace it.

## Testing whether the rope still holds

A practical response is a small investigation rather than a grand promise.

**First, name the prediction precisely.** Replace “Nothing works” with “When I revise by rereading, I still cannot solve these equations.” A specific statement gives you something to examine.

**Second, choose one change you can influence.** Ask for a worked example, practise a smaller skill, change partners, request feedback, or identify someone who can help remove a barrier. Influence does not mean complete control.

**Third, look for evidence, including partial progress.** Did you start sooner? Understand one step? Ask a clearer question? If the result was disappointing, examine the method and conditions before turning it into a judgment about your identity.

This is not a demand to persist forever. Resting, changing a goal, leaving an unsafe situation, or getting adult help may be sensible choices. Some barriers really are strong. They may require collective action, additional resources, or professional support. If feeling powerless is persistent or overwhelming, talking with a trusted adult or school counsellor is itself a meaningful step.

The elephant picture asks whether an old prediction still fits. Keller's story asks whether the method gives someone access. Kamkwamba's asks what knowledge and help could make an attempt possible. Together they suggest a kind of courage quieter than heroic certainty: being willing to investigate whether one thing could be different.

**Pause and reflect:** Choose one fictional school example above. What is the person's prediction? What needs to change around them? What small, fair experiment could provide new evidence? You can discuss the example without sharing a personal struggle.

**A question to carry into the week:** What would help me find out whether “I cannot” really means “I cannot yet, in this way, without this support”?
