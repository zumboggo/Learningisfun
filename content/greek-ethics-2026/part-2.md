# Greek Ethics, Part 2 — Can a Good City Make Good People?

## 1. The ring that removes the witnesses

A shepherd discovers a ring. When he turns it, he becomes invisible. Soon he uses this power to gain access to the palace, kill the king, and take control. This is the **Ring of Gyges**, a story presented by Glaucon in Plato's *Republic*. It is a philosophical thought experiment, not a historical report. Glaucon asks whether apparently just people would remain just if they could escape discovery and punishment. He wants a strong defense of justice, so he gives its opponent a powerful argument. [Plato, *Republic*, Book 2, 359–360](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/republic.3.ii.html).

Imagine a modern version: an official can secretly give a valuable contract to a friend. Nobody will ever find out. If the only reason to act fairly is fear of punishment, fairness has lost its support. But perhaps the action damages something else: the official's character, other people's opportunities, or the trust on which a community depends. Those are different reasons. Which survives even when the official benefits and feels no guilt?

## 2. Plato: justice inside and outside us

**Plato**, a student of Socrates, founded the **Academy**, a community of philosophical study. His dialogues use characters and arguments to explore difficult questions. We should not treat every statement made by a character as Plato's own final opinion. Nevertheless, the *Republic* develops an influential answer: **justice** is more than appearing respectable or following rules under supervision. It concerns the organization of both a person and a city. [Background: *Plato*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/plato/).

The dialogue distinguishes **reason**, which considers what is best; **spirit**, associated with anger, pride, and standing up for oneself; and **appetite**, our desires for such things as food, possessions, and pleasure. A just person has these elements working in an ordered relationship, with reason guiding the whole. Wanting things is not automatically wrong. Trouble arises when a desire takes command regardless of every other consideration. An invisible wrongdoer may escape the police while becoming ruled by an appetite that cannot say “enough.” [*Republic*, Book 4](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/republic.5.iv.html).

The political proposal is striking. Plato's ideal city has rulers trained to understand the good, defenders, and producers. The **philosopher-ruler** should seek the community's good rather than wealth or applause. Plato also argues that suitably capable women should receive the education needed to serve among the guardians. This challenged conventional gender roles, although his city was far from a society of equal individual freedom. [*Republic*, Book 5](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/republic.6.v.html).

The attraction is understandable: we want people with knowledge and sound judgment making consequential decisions. But expertise about what is good is harder to verify than expertise about repairing a roof. How would citizens distinguish a wise ruler from a confident person who merely claims wisdom? And if disagreement is treated as ignorance, what protects the person who notices that the ruler is wrong?

## 3. Education or control?

Plato's famous **cave allegory** pictures prisoners who mistake shadows for reality. Someone released from the cave gradually learns to see more clearly. Education changes what a person can recognize as true. Yet the educated person must return to help govern rather than simply enjoy private enlightenment. Knowledge brings responsibility. [*Republic*, Book 7](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/republic.8.vii.html).

There is a disturbing companion to this vision. The proposed guardians' education restricts stories that might form undesirable habits or emotions. Plato recognizes that narratives shape character, but gives rulers considerable control over them. **Censorship**, restricting what people may encounter or express, can be defended as protection and criticized as domination. The question is not whether education influences people: it inevitably does. It is who chooses its goals, and whether those goals can themselves be challenged. [*Republic*, Book 3](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/republic.4.iii.html).

## 4. Aristotle: becoming good by doing

**Aristotle** studied at Plato's Academy and later established the **Lyceum**. He developed another lasting approach to ethics, centered on **eudaimonia**: flourishing, or living well across a whole life. This is more than having a cheerful mood. A flourishing life involves excellent activity. Aristotle also recognizes that friendship, resources, and good fortune affect how well a life can go. That matters politically: conditions outside an individual's control can help or obstruct living well. [Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*, Book 1](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.1.i.html).

For Aristotle, **virtue** is a developed excellence of character. We become fair through practicing fair actions, much as musicians improve through playing. Yet mechanical repetition is insufficient: a virtuous person eventually acts knowingly, chooses the action for its own sake, and acts from a stable character. His **doctrine of the mean** locates many virtues between failures of excess and deficiency. Courage falls between cowardice and rashness. It is not a mathematical midpoint or advice to compromise about everything. Some actions, including theft, are wrong rather than candidates for a moderate amount. [*Nicomachean Ethics*, Book 2](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.2.ii.html).

**Practical wisdom**, or **phronēsis**, helps a person judge what a particular situation requires. In this invented example, a student challenges a popular friend's cruel joke. Speaking immediately might protect its target; speaking privately might make the friend listen. A general instruction to be courageous does not decide which response fits. Good judgment needs attention to people, timing, likely effects, and reasons. It is more than finding an efficient way to get whatever you want. [*Nicomachean Ethics*, Book 6](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.6.vi.html).

## 5. The limits of their good cities

Aristotle calls human beings political animals: our capacities develop in shared life. In his analysis of governments, rule for a common advantage differs from rule serving only the rulers. Yet he also defends natural slavery and male authority within the household. These positions expose a serious limit in whose flourishing his political order supports. Explaining that limit historically does not justify it. [*Politics*, Books 1 and 3](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/politics.1.one.html).

Plato and Aristotle helped make character, education, and human flourishing central to ethics. Their influence also leaves us a problem: **how much power should a community have to shape its members into good people?** Return to the invisible official. Would better habits solve the problem, or would we still need rules, scrutiny, and opportunities to challenge authority? A strong answer must explain both what education can accomplish and where its authority should end.
