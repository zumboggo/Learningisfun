# Historical Text: stories, ideas, and power

This is the Historical Text mode of Create LIF Text, formerly Ethics Text. Follow the parent skill for audience, length, vocabulary, illustrations, and delivery. Use this mode across classes for events, institutions, and histories of ideas in ethics, literature, science, religion, and other subjects. Ethics is one possible subject, not a required frame.

Favor facts and ideas with emotional resonance, practical significance, surprise, or enduring human interest. Seek stories students might remember years after forgetting the lesson: extremes of courage, loyalty, compassion, humiliation, endurance, and power. Let concrete human experience carry the ideas. Do not force every topic into catastrophe or manufacture melodrama to make it memorable.

Confronting historical facts and occasional graphic detail are appropriate when essential to understanding the human stakes for the intended audience. Avoid gratuitous suffering, invented interior thoughts, and treating costly sacrifice as proof of virtue. Keep moral complexity without manufacturing uncertainty about established harms.

Automatically choose one of the two voices below according to the material. Use A when suspense, individual decisions, scale, or unfolding consequences carry the story. Use B when documented hypocrisy, institutional absurdity, self-serving rules, or failures of accountability provide a natural comic target. If neither has a clear advantage, choose A. Do not ask the user to select a voice unless their request requires it. Some humor can appear in A; B must allow serious passages to remain serious. Do not impose the same rhetorical structure on both.

### Voice A: Conversational storytelling at an enormous scale

- Speak like a knowledgeable person telling an intelligent friend a remarkable story. Use contractions and ordinary language. Let interest show without repeatedly announcing that events are extraordinary.
- Open with a specific predicament: an order, a defeat, an inherited promise, a dangerous statement. Establish why the next action matters.
- Name people, places, roles, and practical constraints. Explain what an office allowed its holder to do. Replace vague references to power with particular powers: arrest, confiscation, appointments, punishment of relatives. Include reliable numbers when they clarify the stakes.
- Move between an individual and the larger machinery affecting them. Return to the person before background becomes a survey of a dynasty.
- Follow the causal chain closely: an event creates pressure, someone responds, and that response produces the next development. Explain unfamiliar motives and institutions at the moment they become necessary.
- Use brief conversational asides to translate unfamiliar situations, acknowledge surprises, or clarify scale, then return to the story.
- Use rhetorical questions or hypophora to express curiosity and carry the explanation forward. Answer with evidence or clearly qualified interpretations rather than handing explanatory work to the reader.
- Let humor arise from vanity, resentment, bad planning, or the gap between grand ambitions and ordinary behavior. Do not turn every scene into a joke.
- Explain why people obeyed, what refusal cost, and whether anyone could challenge a decision. Show how personal irritation could become a public emergency.
- End on a consequence, revealing detail, or unfinished historical development rather than a moral speech or reader question.

### Voice B: Conversational storytelling with an eye for institutional absurdity

- Speak like a well-informed friend explaining how an outrageous arrangement actually worked. Be direct, funny, and specific. Trust readers to recognize cruelty without repeated declarations.
- Open with a documented action or arrangement whose absurdity becomes clear once explained plainly. Avoid general openings about power or human nature.
- Identify who decided, who benefited, who carried it out, and who paid. Translate official euphemisms into ordinary language.
- Establish the facts before the joke. Explain the rule, show its effects, and expose the contradiction. Humor should improve understanding of the institution.
- Aim humor at powerful people's excuses, inflated self-images, convenient exceptions, and failures of accountability. Never make victims, unfamiliar names, or cultural differences the joke.
- Prefer a short, dry observation after a concrete fact. Avoid stacking punchlines or forcing a joke into every paragraph.
- Use modern comparisons only when they clarify incentives or relationships; acknowledge meaningful limits. Avoid repetitive corporate or social-media analogies.
- Explain who could refuse, appeal, check evidence, or correct a ruler. Answer through the story, not a worksheet.
- Call cruelty or dishonesty what it is when supported. Keep uncertainty where evidence is uncertain, without automatic balancing or repeated lists of "perhaps" alternatives.
- Explain motives beyond stupidity or wickedness. An intelligent ruler can make disastrous decisions; useful institutions can protect abusive officials.
- Let humor stop during serious suffering without announcing the tonal change. End on a consequence or a contradiction that survives the official explanation, not an attached sermon.

Use these as flexible modes, not imitations of living authors or hosts. Do not reproduce their distinctive phrasing, catchphrases, or personas. Voice must preserve accessibility, source accuracy, and room for student judgment.

For comparisons, follow the user's choice of same or different stories. Otherwise, use one episode when testing voice alone and suitable distinct episodes when demonstrating automatic voice selection. Honor the requested sample length, verify exact word counts when requested, and exclude titles and citations from those counts.

## Ground the reading

Verify historical details and quotations using available sources; browse for material not supplied. Prefer primary texts, identifiable translations, museums, universities, and scholarly reference works. Link sources near relevant passages and include a concise source list. Observe quotation limits. Never invent quotations, laws, provenance, or citations.

Distinguish historical evidence, modern interpretation, paraphrase, and imagined cases. Label invented dialogue and thought experiments explicitly. Explain uncertainty when evidence is incomplete. Avoid treating a civilisation or tradition as one voice, projecting modern categories backward without explanation, or presenting history as a simple march from primitive to enlightened thought.

Students should be able to distinguish what a source reports from what it argues, and from reasons they themselves propose. Supply enough evidence to make that distinction possible.

## Write an open but substantive text

1. Open with a concrete problem, puzzling claim, short passage, object, or consequential decision. Establish the stakes without manufacturing melodrama.
2. Give the context and vocabulary students need. Define important terms in the flow of the prose. Keep a coherent thread rather than surveying everything about the topic.
3. Supply specific passages or details students can cite. Number paragraphs if it helps discussion in the requested format.
4. Present plausible competing reasons fairly. A serious difficulty can be an unclear concept, disputed interpretation, competing duty, missing premise, or consequence—not necessarily a dramatic dilemma.
5. Explain what happened and how events and ideas connect. Do not repeatedly state the lesson after each example or force an unresolved ending. Leave students room to form their own judgments without obscuring facts to create artificial uncertainty.
6. Avoid obvious villains, deliberately weak principles, forced false balance, and choices where the desired answer is signalled by loaded wording. A morally objectionable practice can still prompt inquiry into its justification, assumptions, and implications without pretending it is equally defensible.
7. Where useful, introduce one changed circumstance that exposes a relevant distinction. Do not overload a short reading with unrelated cases.

Do not require personal disclosures or reward costly sacrifice as proof of moral quality. Do not insist that all ethical principles are empirically falsifiable. Sources supply claims and reasons to examine, not automatic moral authority.

## Keep the discussion routine outside the text

Deliver the story and ideas, not an embedded lesson or worksheet. Do not append discussion prompts, ideas for examination, comprehension questions, sentence starters, portfolio instructions, suggested takeaways, or teacher notes unless explicitly requested. Rhetorical questions and hypophora (asking a question and answering it) are welcome as storytelling devices. Do not address questions to the reader to answer, including a final discussion question disguised as a narrative ending.

Make authority understandable and open to challenge through evidence: show what power permits, what limits exist, and what happens when limits fail. Do not predetermine students' political judgments, conceal inconvenient evidence, or make every authority figure a cartoon villain.

Avoid habitual signposts such as "This raises an important question," "That deserves examination," "Here is where ethics becomes useful," and "Notice the tension." Avoid repeated paragraphs explaining the significance of the previous paragraph. Let students' own discussion routine handle explicit examination.

## Delivery and quality check

Return a title, student reading, and concise source links. Keep sources outside the story where practical, with citations near claims when needed. Keep voice labels outside student prose when useful for a comparison. Do not add teacher notes, rubrics, or lesson plans unless requested.

Before delivering, check:
- Does the material offer a memorable human detail or a worthwhile practical or intellectual stake without sensationalism?
- Is course vocabulary reused accurately and naturally, with unfamiliar terms explained?
- Does the chosen voice support inquiry rather than decide the student's judgment through tone?
- Can a student quote a specific passage to examine the central issue?
- Is there enough context to understand it without outside expertise?
- Is the remaining work substantive rather than recall or agreement with the narrator?
- Can a multilingual teenager follow how each development leads to the next without outside expertise?
- Have I removed discussion prompts, examination instructions, suggested takeaways, and questions addressed to the reader?
- Does the ending belong to the story rather than explain its moral?
- Are historical claims verified and invented material labelled?
- Have I left room for defended agreement, qualification, rejection, or reasoned uncertainty?
- Does the reading serve the requested class and topic without forcing an Ethics and Leadership framing onto another subject?
