# Create LIF Text

Create compelling student readings for Learning Is Fun (LIF). Historical Text is the first specialized mode, formerly Ethics Text. Use it for historical narratives and histories of ideas across classes, including ethics and philosophy. For other reading forms, follow the requested form and these shared standards without imposing a historical or ethical frame.

## Audience and voice

Use the requested class, topic, sources, dates, and constraints. Default to David's mixed-attainment high-school audience with many multilingual learners. Clear language should preserve intellectual difficulty. Explain unfamiliar offices, relationships, terms, and motives when they become necessary. Make each event or idea lead clearly to the next.

Read [Historical Text](references/historical-text.md) for historical readings and the detailed voice descriptions. Automatically choose **Voice A**, conversational storytelling at an enormous scale, when individual decisions, suspense, scale, or unfolding consequences carry the account. Choose **Voice B**, conversational storytelling with an eye for institutional absurdity, when documented hypocrisy, self-serving rules, or failures of accountability naturally support dry humor. Default to A when neither clearly fits better. Explicit user choices take precedence. These are original modes, not imitations of named living hosts. For another requested form, adapt the compatible conversational qualities without forcing satire or a historical narrative.

## Standard length and pictures

- Default to **about 20 minutes**, planning for **1,500–1,800 words of student prose and four original illustrations per reading**. This assumes thoughtful multilingual reading at roughly 80–95 words per minute with pauses for pictures and unfamiliar ideas. It is a planning estimate, not a universal speed or timed guarantee.
- Explicit lengths override this default. A 10–15-minute reading can be approximately 800–1,100 words. Brief samples follow their requested length. Exclude titles, sources, and captions from prose counts; verify exact counts only when exactness was requested, otherwise check the target range.
- Generate **four built-in ImageGen pictures** for a normal full reading, placed at distinct meaningful points. Brief samples, text-only requests, and small edits do not automatically need new images. Preserve good existing illustrations when revising.
- Read the installed imagegen skill when generating. After the facts and narrative structure are stable, plan the scenes together: setting, subject, emotional purpose, evocative style, and historical constraints. Generate one asset per call; a collage does not count as four separate pictures. Do not silently switch to a paid CLI/API workflow.
- Use coherent art direction with varied compositions. Show consequential people, objects, encounters, or environments. Avoid decorative repetition, gratuitous violence, fake archival evidence, and invented writing in the artwork. Generated scenes are interpretations, not evidence.
- Inspect each output for relevance, obvious historical mismatches, confusing imagery, and legibility. Regenerate only for a concrete problem. Save final assets in the deliverable folder with stable names, meaningful alt text, and short captions identifying imagined/AI-generated scenes. Keep a compact prompt-to-file record. Do not leave project assets only at the generation tool's default location.

## Vocabulary and memorable material

Consult the relevant class section of `../vocab-presentation/assets/CORE_VOCAB_BY_WEEK.txt`, if available. Prioritize an explicitly requested week, then naturally relevant yearly terms. Do not infer teaching sequence from the calendar or use Ethics vocabulary automatically for another class. If there is no matching list, use supplied vocabulary and subject-essential terms without inventing a course list.

Introduce terms when the story gives them meaning, explain them briefly in context, and allow natural recurrence. Compelling human experience takes precedence over covering every vocabulary word. Distinguish comparisons from historical equivalences and modern analytical language from actors' own concepts.

Favor reliable details with emotional resonance, surprise, practical value, or enduring interest. Include at least one developed story in a full historical reading. For a series, plan each part's story, concepts, voice, and four pictures together to avoid repetition and gaps. Keep parts independently readable with brief connective context.

## Student text and delivery

Deliver a title, engaging prose, illustrations, and concise source links. Verify claims and quotations; distinguish evidence, later teaching stories, interpretation, and invented examples. Prefer primary texts and reputable scholarly or institutional context. Research shared sources once for a series, then verify uncertain claims directly.

Do not append discussion prompts, examination ideas, comprehension questions, portfolio tasks, suggested morals, or teacher notes unless requested. Rhetorical questions and hypophora can carry the narration; do not ask the reader to supply an answer. Let the class's existing discussion routine do that work.

For full illustrated readings, save an editable source with final images. Create additional formats only when requested or useful for the destination, not several redundant versions by habit. A draft request does not authorize publication. For requested LIF publication or assignment, read [LIF delivery](references/lif-delivery.md). Do not add Canvas assignments unless requested.

## Efficient completion

Check facts, voice, causal clarity, vocabulary, word count, image count, source links, and absence of reader tasks. Check once at each meaningful stage; repeat only after changes or failures. Use focused tool results rather than full documents, browser trees, or credential output. Inspect a representative live reader layout and verify every reading and image through the available data interfaces. Report files or live links, assignment dates when applicable, and genuine limitations.
