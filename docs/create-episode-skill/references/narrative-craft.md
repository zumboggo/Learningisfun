# Narrative craft

Outline Harmon’s You → Need → Go → Search → Find → Take → Return → Change: concrete role, desired outcome, magical threshold, investigation/action, apparent solution, cost/complication, resolution/safe return, demonstrated understanding. These are authoring beats, not eight required screens.

Apply two Lisa Cron principles: prior experience/belief changes what an event means to a character; scenes follow cause → interpretation → action → consequence. Give each important NPC a want, concern and belief, shown in action rather than biography. Establish care before tradeoffs. Do not impose moral beliefs on the real student.

Write first-person present, warm humour and one or two sentences per beat. Introduce place/time, role and interlocutor clearly. Student is their own adventurer with a useful task; preserve literary/historical canonical events. Usually five to seven choices; major commitments fit original decision-led adventures. In source-led literary episodes, small local choices can be meaningful through character reactions and attention without changing the plot. Curiosity informs action. Branches reconnect with remembered evidence, promises and relationships. Keep the speaking turn clear; show two characters together for conversations and physical exchanges. Alternate wide action views and closer reaction shots. Use action captions instead of paragraphs describing movement the stage can show. Plan fixed source events first, then place brief reconverging student choices between them. Only the opening disclaimer breaks immersion.

Use actual weekly vocabulary naturally. Knowledge must matter to the problem: a qualification changes a promise; a source resolves uncertainty. Chance and other people's motives can upset sound choices; foreshadow risks, retain agency after setbacks, and save chance outcomes. The student never dies; consequences stay inside the episode, though others can face serious non-graphic losses. Assess four knowledge questions independently from narrative luck. Optional writing is separate.

Review a whole path aloud, checking clear role, short explanations, causal progression, emotional costs and resolution. Test a defensible action with bad luck and a weak action with good luck.

## Three writing passes

### 1. Opening check

Before expanding the draft, state the episode's promise in one sentence: the student's concrete role and want, the obstacle, and what could be lost. Read the opening as a first-time player. Can they identify where they are, whom they are dealing with, and why the immediate problem matters? Bring the promised conflict or intriguing discrepancy into the first short scene; avoid a long classroom briefing or historical lecture before anything happens. This is a pacing goal, not a fixed line count or a requirement for danger.

Reveal background when it helps the student understand or choose. A cold open is optional; it must not confuse chronology, invent source events, or remove essential context for multilingual readers. Preserve the Teaching Stone/magical Didi/journal framing, but keep the transition economical. Establish enough care and evidence before asking for a consequential decision. In an Ethics story, dramatize the competing demands before explaining their philosophical labels.

### 2. Choice–response–callback plan

Before scripting branches, use one compact row per choice (in the working outline, not student-facing text):

| Choice ID | Student intention/options | Immediate response for each option | Remembered fact and later payoff | Rejoin point |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| approach | Ask privately / question publicly | Mentor lowers their guard / crowd hears and mentor becomes defensive | `private` boolean; later thanks for discretion / request to correct the public accusation | Shared evidence scene |

Every option should communicate what the student intends and deliver an observable difference in dialogue, information, action or another character's response. Keep the wording clear about the action; do not promise a result the student cannot control. Avoid a button that sounds considerate but unexpectedly makes the protagonist cruel. Uncertain outcomes need intelligible risks and causes.

Rejoin branches to control workload, while retaining the local consequence. Small expressive choices may end with an immediate reaction; mark their later payoff as none. Important commitments should return when relevant through a remembered fact, relationship response or material consequence. Never pretend identical outcomes are a major branching decision. For fixed literary events, let choices change approach, understanding or reactions without promising to change the canonical event.

Use the existing maximum of four bounded variables/toggles and supported `if`/`goto` syntax. Save only facts with a planned use; do not introduce a variable for every choice or imply cross-episode memory exists. Callback performances use supported scene beats/emotions/actions. Keep narrative relationships separate from knowledge scores. Compare each option's actual branch and check its later callback, including after resume.

### 3. Scene-trimming pass

After the draft, name what changes in each scene: knowledge, intention, relationship, risk or the practical situation. If nothing changes, combine or cut the scene unless it earns its place as a brief emotional pause or a clear transition. Start close to the interesting exchange and leave after its turn; remove repeated arrivals, greetings, explanations and conclusions.

At line level, ask whether the text advances action, reveals character, clarifies a necessary fact, or gives a consequence room to land. Remove narration that merely repeats a visible supported gesture. Keep essential dialogue, context and accessible captions: the student must still understand the scene with reduced motion or static-image fallback. Do not describe props or eye movements as rendered if the runtime only narrates or mimes them.

Read one complete route aloud and compare a contrasting route. Check that trimming has preserved motives, source fidelity, information needed for decisions, emotional costs and the ending's payoff. Prefer fewer purposeful gestures to constant animation. Finish the episode's central problem; a lingering ethical question can invite reflection without withholding the promised resolution.

### Sources and adaptation

These passes adapt Episode Interactive's guidance to short educational adventures; they are not Episode's scripting syntax or a promise of increased engagement. No paid choices or romance requirement is adopted.

- [Angela Han, 11 Tips to Writing a Hit Episode Story](https://pocketgems-support.helpshift.com/hc/en/10-episode-writer-s-portal/faq/234-11-tips-to-writing-a-hit-episode-story/): premise delivery, character motivation, concise scenes and timely exposition.
- [Episode, Tips for Writing Great Choices](https://pocketgems-support.helpshift.com/hc/en/10-episode-writer-s-portal/faq/451-tips-for-writing-great-choices/): informed choices, distinct responses and honoring the selected intention.
- [Dara Amarie, Remember a Choice](https://www.dara-amarie.com/remember-choice): conditional callbacks; adapt the pattern to our own variable system.
- [Joseph Evans, Top 5 Tips](https://www.episodelife.com/2016/08/top-5-tips-for-writing-an-awesome-episode-story.html): manageable reconverging branches. Our adaptation requires a real local consequence rather than only an appearance of agency.
