The Stolen Camel

Ethics from China to Islam · first-person historical episode · week of November 16, 2026
10–12 minutes of story, then four knowledge questions and a short Decision Note.

Primary focus: 4b.1 — Construct an argument.
Objective: connect a judgement to reasons and explain how a detail supports it. Success: a clear judgement, one accurate case detail, an explained connection, and a serious alternative reason. Choices do not score moral goodness.

Case and teaching boundaries

The student travels as themself, becomes a guest of a Muzayna household, and helps bring accounts to a hearing. The episode develops the owner’s loss alongside the accused people’s lack of freedom and food. Umar’s reported decision remains fixed across choices. The source is Malik’s later transmitted Muwatta, Judgements 36.38 / Arabic 1441, not a contemporary transcript. Do not identify this incident automatically with the separate year-of-famine accounts.

Malik preserves the judgement but distinguishes it from his community’s rule on compensation. His comment gives students an accessible example of argument within Islamic jurisprudence. Slavery is named as coercion; neither the source nor this episode establishes emancipation or a completed payment. The threatened bodily punishment is mentioned briefly without a visual depiction.

All learner encounters, Salim, dialogue, the bowl and tether, and the exact setting are authored staging. The report’s sequence from threatened punishment to starvation and financial liability supports the traditional reading of non-application; do not present a fuller invented courtroom transcript as evidence. Umar is encountered through narrated decisions.

Connect to What Do We Owe Each Other?, the unit’s first reading. Qur’an 9:60 names beneficiaries of obligatory giving; 2:280 addresses debtors in hardship. A compensation payment is distinct from a loan. Graeber’s modern argument invites students to question identifying morality entirely with paying debts. It is a comparison, not an influence on Umar or proof that these people were enslaved through debt.

Student support and Decision Note

Recap: someone lost property; the accused lacked food and freedom; the authority considered the enslaver’s responsibility; a financial order did not establish that all needs had been met.

Essential words: caliph — community leader after Muhammad; Qur’an — Islam’s scripture; zakat — obligatory giving; compensation — payment for loss; jurist — scholar of law; obligation — something a person owes or is required to do. Monetary debt and wider obligations can overlap without being identical.

Answer the first two prompts, then choose one of the three third-question options. One or two sentences per answer are enough. For “the character,” name the person whose decision you are judging, including the first-person visitor. Do not assume every person has the same freedom to act.

  1. What should the character do? Why? “They should ___ because ___.” Include one episode detail.
  2. Why might someone choose differently? “Someone might choose ___ because ___.” Take that reason seriously.
  3. Choose one:
    • What if the accused had enough food and took the camel to sell for profit? Would your judgement change? Why?
    • Can the owner be owed compensation even when the accused should not receive the threatened punishment? Why?
    • Who should bear the cost of the lost camel? Support your answer with one detail and explain why it matters.

Completion with revision: relevant attempts using a case detail, not agreement with a preferred position or polished English. Writing stays separate from gameplay. The four knowledge questions award 25 app points each; 75 is the completion threshold with review/retry. These are not automatic Canvas grades.

Optional fortnightly presentation · solo or pairs

Develop this Decision Note or the previous week’s. Same task and workload for solo/pair work; 4–6 minutes including a brief audience choice, in mixed circles of about four students. Both partners contribute. Keep slides concise; put assessable reasoning in notes.

SlideVisible content and speaker notes
1. Story and choiceAn image or vivid dilemma and audience choice. Notes explain context, accurate details and source.
2. Principle and reasonsInitial principle and a few cues. Notes connect a claim to evidence (4b.1), explain the connection, include the strongest alternative and an accurate course connection.
3. CounterexampleLabel a hypothetical changed circumstance. Notes explain why it challenges the same principle. Optional cue: enough food, theft for profit. Advanced students may expose an assumption or devise a harder test.
4. Revised or defended principleA revised principle or explicit retention. Notes explain how it handles both cases and one limitation; add an audience objection and response afterwards.

Optional starters: “When deciding responsibility, we should consider ___”; “Repairing harm requires ___.” These are openings, not finished arguments. Students may defend an original principle. About 200–300 words of speaker notes is guidance, not a quota. Each student adds a labelled 40–60-word personal note to slide 4: a reason they endorse, what they reconsidered or defended, and their contribution. Submit PPTX with notes or accessible equivalent plus notes export; a slides-only PDF misses evidence.

Teacher-only marking cues

Grade visible reasoning in the submitted notes, not presumed confidence, accent, delivery or agreement. If Canvas hides notes, download the original. Five criteria, each 0 absent/mistaken, 1 partial, 2 clear and supported: accurate case + 4b.1; supported judgement; fair alternative; genuine counterexample and justified response; individual reasoning. First four may share a pair score; last is individual. Total 10.

Two plausible paths: an argument centred on the duty-holder’s responsibility for deprivation; an argument centred on securing repair for the owner while distinguishing inability to pay and culpability. Neither path is a supplied student answer. A strong response makes the missing inference explicit rather than merely listing facts.

Likely misconception: “Hunger means property no longer matters,” or “800 dirhams settles every duty.” Ask what claim the fact supports and what it leaves unanswered. Feedback prompt: “How does that detail support your proposed responsibility, rather than simply describe what happened?” Give one strength and one next step. The story alone does not assess implemented service or actual leadership.
