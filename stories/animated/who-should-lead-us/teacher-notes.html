Who Should Lead Us?

Ethics and Leadership · Due Friday, November 27, 2026
About 12 minutes of story, four knowledge questions, and three short reflection answers. This additional Friday episode preserves Tuesday’s The Road into the Plague.

Focus and success

1b.1 — Explain and evaluate why a person holding a studied worldview might choose differently. Student objective: I can explain why people committed to Muhammad’s guidance could disagree about rightful leadership. Success: give a specific reason for each position, support a judgement with an episode detail, and recognise a cost or limit. Grade fair reasoning, not agreement with a religious position.

Case recap and historical limits

After Muhammad’s death in Medina in 632, the Ansar gather at Saqifa. Abu Bakr, Umar and Abu Ubayda join them; Ali is absent. Allegiance is pledged to Abu Bakr, who becomes the first caliph. The student helps two neighbours carry questions, news and food while deciding what advice and assistance they can responsibly offer.

Hisham’s strongest case: Abu Bakr’s trusted companionship, early service, reported prayer leadership and recognised judgement support a leader under whom the community can preserve its faith and act together. Bashir’s strongest case: Ali’s knowledge, service and understood designation at Ghadir make leadership an entrusted responsibility which a hurried gathering cannot simply transfer. Family relationship is part of this argument, not a substitute for qualification or designation.

Neither side is indifferent to faith or community. Consultation is important but not a guaranteed solution to disputed designation. The Ansar have their own claims, so this is not a modern two-candidate election. Sunni tradition honours Ali while recognising Abu Bakr’s succession; Shia traditions uphold Ali’s designated right and the special role of the Prophet’s family. Later events, including Karbala in 680, and generations of thought shape diverse traditions. Do not imply all later differences or inevitable conflict were settled in 632.

Teacher provenance: Hisham, Bashir, their dialogue and local tasks are authored, not recovered eyewitness testimony. Historical leaders are narrated without invented direct quotations or portraits. Later reports differ on sensitive details, including Ali’s allegiance chronology. The episode does not claim to settle those disputes. The family’s absence matters without staging contested violence as established fact.

Sources and a useful reading connection

  • Bukhari 6830: Umar’s retrospective account of Saqifa, urgency and consultation. Use its succession portion; the page also contains unrelated penal-law material.
  • Bukhari 683: prayer-leadership report invoked by Hisham. Distinguish the report from the disputed inference that it establishes succession.
  • Tirmidhi 3713: the mawla declaration. Harvard’s Ghadir discussion explains its importance and interpretive disagreements. Do not settle the issue by treating one English gloss as conclusive.
  • Institute of Ismaili Studies: a situated Shia account of spiritual guidance and designation, not every Shia community’s position.
  • Bukhari 4240–41: exclusion complaint and later reconciliation in this report; not the dialogue of the day at Saqifa.
  • OpenStax historical overview: development of traditions and later events.

Reading idea: Umar’s later account treats the sudden pledge as exceptional while warning against bypassing consultation. In paraphrase, an outcome’s acceptance need not make its process a general rule. Compare this with the designation argument that some duties exist before a gathering accepts them.

Concepts: succession: taking leadership after another person; caliph: successor as community leader, not a new prophet; allegiance: a commitment of support; legitimacy: rightful authority; Ansar: Medina’s Muslim supporters; designation: identifying someone for a role. Mawla has several meanings; interpretation depends on context. These are episode concepts, not an asserted weekly vocabulary list.

Principles Portfolio — three short answers

  1. What should the character do? Why?
    “They should ___ because ___.” Choose Hisham or Bashir and include one episode detail.
  2. Why might someone choose differently?
    “Someone might choose ___ because ___.” Give a reason the person could recognise as fair.
  3. Choose ONE question:
    • How can Hisham and Bashir share a commitment to Muhammad’s guidance yet disagree about who should lead?
    • What if Ali and the Prophet’s family had attended the gathering? Would that settle the disagreement about rightful authority? Why?
    • Can helping neighbours alongside a leader’s supporters be different from accepting that leader’s authority? Explain using the episode.

One or two sentences each is enough. The “What if” deliberately changes a historical circumstance. Support starters: “A decision is rightful when ___”; “Shared work does not necessarily mean ___.” Students may defend or revise a principle. Do not supply completed student answers or require personal religious disclosure. Writing remains separate from gameplay; existing notebook assignment controls remain opt-in.

Knowledge check

Four questions test the Saqifa outcome, each position’s strongest argument, and the relationship to later Sunni/Shia traditions. Each is worth 25 app points; three correct passes with review/retry. No choice of allegiance or moral opinion earns points. App scores do not automatically become Canvas grades.

Fortnightly presentation guidance

When requested, students present solo or in pairs with equal status, 4–6 minutes including a brief audience choice. Four slides, with reasoning in speaker notes (roughly 200–300 words across the deck as guidance, not a quota):

  1. Case and decision: enough context to judge; identify the source and ask the audience to choose.
  2. Principle and reasons: specific detail, serious opposing reason, and how shared commitments can produce different judgements.
  3. Counterexample: explicitly hypothetical change to attendance, evidence of designation, or risk of division; test the same principle.
  4. Revised or defended principle: explain its limit and add an audience objection and response after presenting.

Each student adds a labelled 40–60-word individual note to slide 4 on their own judgement, reconsideration/defence and contribution. Submit notes with slides; a slides-only PDF is insufficient. This companion does not create a separate presentation assignment.

Teacher-only marking cues

Weekly reflections: completion with revision for relevant answers, an episode detail and a fair alternative. Presentation if assigned: five criteria at 0–2 each—accurate case and 1b.1; supported judgement; fair alternative; counterexample and response; individual reasoning. Assess the submitted artifact, not presumed oral confidence or accent.

Two defensible reasoning paths: support common leadership based on trusted service and urgent coordination while recognising exclusion and obligations to dissenters; or withhold allegiance on a designation/rightful-authority principle while recognising disputed evidence and practical duties to neighbours. Broader consultation can also be defended if its limits and time costs are addressed. No position automatically earns full marks.

Watch for: “Sunni equals democracy; Shia equals nepotism”; “winning proves legitimacy”; “the loser simply wanted power”; “working together proves agreement”; “all Sunnis/Shias believe exactly the same things”; “the schism happened completely in one meeting.”

Feedback: “You identify ___ as a shared commitment. Explain why it supports different conclusions for these two people, then acknowledge the strongest challenge to your preferred conclusion.” Advanced work tests an assumption rather than merely adding length.

Suitability: recognisable immediate duties; substantial competing claims; common factual recap on all routes; six choices with consequential callbacks; no religious test, morality score or promised counterfactual historical victory.
