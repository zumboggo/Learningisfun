The Sheep Behind Our House

Help Jun respond when his father steals a neighbour’s sheep. Six choices explore family loyalty, truth, privacy and repair; returning the animal does not end every harm. Approximately 10–12 minutes plus a four-question knowledge check; story choices are unscored.

Primary standard 1b.1
Explain and evaluate how a studied worldview can lead people to different responses to wrongdoing.
Success: Represent the Confucian claim of family loyalty accurately, explain a serious competing claim, and evaluate a response using a specific detail; no preferred answer about reporting is graded.

Before playing

Use Part 1, The Teacher and the Tiger, and Part 2, The Job Offer and the Dead Tortoise. The story gives the essential context, so students can begin without outside research. Explain only the terms they need for the next decision. Use pairs or individual play; short spoken explanations are welcome.

Three short answers

  1. What should the character do? Why? “They should ___ because ___.” Use one story detail.
  2. Why might someone choose differently? “Someone might choose ___ because ___.” Give a reason worth taking seriously.
  3. Choose one from the episode dropdown:
    • Can Jun refuse his father’s request and still show filial care? Explain why.
    • What if the sheep’s owner were a stranger to Jun? Would his responsibility change? Why?
    • Does returning the sheep repair all the harm? What else should count, and why?

One or two short sentences per answer are enough. The answers save in the private Ethics Principles Portfolio, separately from the quiz. Do not require a second copy in Canvas. Keeping a judgement with better reasons is as valid as changing it.

Discussion moves

Allow thinking time, then compare a reason with a partner. Ask “Who bears that cost?”, “What do you mean by that?”, or one supplied “What if…?” variation. Ask students to represent an alternative fairly before evaluating it. Avoid asking every student to perform every philosophical move after every episode.

Developing a later presentation

Students may work solo or in pairs, with equal status. Develop one episode response into four slides: (1) case and choice; (2) a principle and reasons, including a serious alternative; (3) a concrete changed detail that tests that principle; (4) a revised principle or a defended original and one remaining difficulty. Reasoning belongs in concise speaker notes; 200–300 words across the deck is guidance, not a quota. Each student adds a labelled 40–60-word personal reasoning/contribution note to slide 4. After presenting, record one audience objection and response. This is a development task, not an additional assignment created by these episodes.

Teacher-only review

Judge relevant reasoning, accurate concepts and use of a detail. Do not reward English polish, length, agreement with a preferred answer or unobserved delivery. Suggested later presentation rubric: five criteria, 0–2 each: accurate case and primary focus; supported judgement; fair alternative; genuine counterexample and justified response; individual reasoning. Grade from slides and notes, reading the original if the preview hides notes.

Common misconception: Filial care always means obedience, or returning property automatically restores trust.

Feedback prompt: How does your answer preserve the strongest reason for family loyalty while facing the owner’s claim?

Costs and plausible reasoning paths — keep out of the initial student task
  • Private settlement protects family privacy but asks Shen to risk trusting a witnessed promise.
  • A record can protect the agreement while exposing the household to lasting stigma and an uncertain hearing.
  • A day’s delay gives time to repair but asks Shen to bear uncertainty.
  • Work, family assets and community aid each move costs to different people; none buys forgiveness.

One defensible path emphasises the immediate duties and harms made visible in the case. Another emphasises the value of a shared practice or relationship, while accepting limits and a concrete way to question or check it. In both cases ask what the preferred response leaves unresolved. No single branch proves a permanent moral character.

Source boundary

Residents, events, conversations, local procedures and outcomes are authored teaching cases. They are not documented incidents or verified ancient legal rules. The initial notice appears before Accept; the story then stays in-world. The source notes in the episode identify the original passages and distinguish them from the case.
