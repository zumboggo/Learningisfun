Dinner with the Tyrant

Can you accept someone’s hospitality without letting them buy your voice?

Seven choices; approximately 10–12 minutes of story, four knowledge questions, then a separate 8–10-minute Decision Note in the app’s Principles Portfolio. Three correct knowledge answers permit completion. No points for choosing a preferred philosophy.

Focus and evidence

1b.1 — Explain and evaluate why a person holding a studied worldview might choose differently. Student objective: explain a Cynic response and an Aristippus-inspired Cyrenaic response to the same offer, then evaluate their reasons using a specific detail. Observable evidence: Decision Note question 3 and the presentation’s reasoning notes. Success: accurately identify both positions, connect each to a different possible action, and explain a genuine strength or limitation of each. Different conclusions and defensible uncertainty can succeed.

Case recap

An ancient anecdote contrasts Diogenes washing vegetables with Aristippus attending rulers’ courts. The Teaching Stone expands that exchange into fiction: Niko, a courier, needs a receipt for fishing families’ petition. Aristippus offers access through an imagined banquet. A public toast asks for extravagant praise; a purse offers useful help alongside an uncertain future obligation. The player chooses how to approach the invitation, endorsement, gift and petition.

Every route ends with food and a receipt, not a repealed fee. Routes differ in claims made, access, cost, exposure and dependence. The next question follows from that achievement: what if the household tomorrow asks the courier to endorse the fee he came to challenge?

Episode concepts, not weekly vocabulary additions:
  • Cynicism: an ancient tradition emphasising virtue, self-sufficiency and challenging conventions. Practising fewer needs can reduce patrons’ power over you. It is different from assuming everyone acts selfishly.
  • Cyrenaic tradition: gives pleasure an important place in a good life. Aristippus’s adaptability and self-command should be distinguished from later systematic Cyrenaic doctrines. Enjoyment does not imply accepting every condition.
  • Patronage: support from someone with wealth or power, potentially creating expectations.
  • Self-sufficiency: needing less external support; it is not a claim that people never need help.
  • Endorsement: expressing approval. Thanking someone for a meal need not endorse all their actions.
  • Tyrant: in this Greek context, a ruler holding personal power outside ordinary constitutional authority.

Five separate Decision Note prompts

Use the app’s familiar five Principles Portfolio fields. About 80–120 words total, or five concise bullets, is guidance rather than a graded quota. Keep the original response for later reconsideration. Writing is separate from the knowledge check and never gates the game.

  1. Choose: What should the visitor or Niko do about the hospitality and gift?
  2. Explain: Give your strongest reason and one specific episode detail.
  3. Explain the other side — focus 1b.1: How could a Cynic and an Aristippus-inspired Cyrenaic reasonably choose differently here? Give the strongest opposing reason and evaluate one cost or limitation of your preferred approach.
  4. State a principle: What general rule supports your judgment? How might it apply elsewhere?
  5. Test it: What meaningful change might make you reconsider that rule? Explain why, or defend the original rule against the challenge.

Optional frames: “This detail matters because…”; “A Cynic might choose… because…”; “Aristippus might respond… because…”; “Someone could reasonably disagree because…”; “My rule becomes difficult when…”

Optional principle starters: “I can accept support when…”; “I should refuse a benefit if…”; “Helping someone through influence is acceptable only when…” These are starting points, not completed answers.

Counterexample cues: the gift requires a false statement; the courier has enough money to refuse comfortably; accepting a gift would compromise someone else’s voice; refusing every form of help would leave a family without necessities. Choose a change that actually tests your own principle. Advanced students can expose a hidden assumption or construct a harder counterexample rather than write more.

Presentation handoff: follow the current live assignment

The updated Canvas task checked October 22 takes precedence over the older four-slide skill template: solo or with one partner, exactly five slides, one main picture on each, 4–6 minutes to the whole class. Use the episode answers to find a different real story from personal experience, news or history. Do not retell this banquet as the student presentation. Anonymity and omission of private details are welcome.

Use the live assignment’s exact five-slide directions. As preparation, organise notes around the real decision, competing reasons, your judgment or principle, a serious challenge or uncertainty, and your response. Make the episode connection explicit: explain why people with different outlooks might respond differently, without assigning Greek-school labels to real people without evidence.

Keep complete reasoning in speaker notes, approximately 200–300 words total as guidance. Each student adds the required named 40–60-word individual note: a personally endorsed reason, response to a challenge and contribution. After presenting, record a genuine audience question and response. Submit the deck with notes or an accessible equivalent including a notes export. Students may retain a principle, revise it or remain precisely uncertain. This guide creates no extra presentation or Canvas task.

Teacher-only marking and discussion cues

Weekly Decision Note: completion with revision for relevant attempts at all five prompts and one specific episode detail. Assess reasons rather than agreement or polished English. Focus feedback: “Which belief explains that different choice, and what does your example reveal about its strength or cost?” Offer one strength and one next step.

Two plausible paths: simplicity and refusing benefits can reduce leverage, but must explain how to help people who cannot choose their poverty. Conditional engagement can deliver food and access while setting limits, but must explain how to recognise dependence and refuse once a gift is spent. Neither route’s local success proves a universal moral rule.

Likely misconceptions: Cynicism is distrust of everyone; Cyrenaicism is Epicureanism or unlimited indulgence; all hospitality buys loyalty; refusing every benefit automatically makes someone virtuous; voluntary simplicity and forced poverty are identical; receiving a petition proves the policy was changed; an invented outcome establishes historical fact.

Current live presentation rubric, 10 points: bring us to the decision (3); explain your judgment (3); make the story accessible (2); show individual understanding (2). The focus informs the reasoning criterion without changing its weight. In pairs, the first three may be shared; individual understanding is separate. Judge evidence in slides and notes, not inferred confidence, accent, artistic polish or agreement with the teacher. Do not replace this with the older five-by-two rubric.

Sources and fidelity

  • Diogenes Laërtius, Aristippus, especially 2.68 for the vegetable exchange; 2.66–69, 2.75, 2.79 and 2.82 for attributed court/self-command anecdotes. Written centuries after the supposed events. Exact occurrence, place and wording are uncertain. The episode paraphrases and invents dialogue; it does not claim authentic quotations.
  • Diogenes Laërtius, Diogenes, especially 6.22–23 and 6.70–71 for training and self-sufficiency; 6.58 preserves a variant of the vegetable story involving Plato.
  • The Cyrenaics, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: distinguishes Aristippus of Cyrene’s outlook from later formulations, including his grandson’s systematic hedonism.

The stone joins an unspecified imagined Greek street to an imagined court in Syracuse. It does not establish that Diogenes visited this banquet or that a historical journey joined the anecdotes. Niko, Philon, the fee, petition, purse, toast, conditions, written note and every local outcome are fictional. Artistic settings are theatrical illustrations, not archaeological reconstructions.
