The Road into the Plague

Ethics and Leadership · Week of November 23, 2026 · Assigned November 24
About 12 minutes of story, four knowledge questions, then three short reflections.

Focus and evidence

1b.1 — Explain and evaluate why a person holding a studied worldview might choose differently. Student objective: I can explain how shared faith can lead people to different judgements about danger and responsibility. Success: a judgement supported by one episode detail, a fair opposing reason, and recognition of a cost or limit. Evaluate reasoning, not agreement with Umar or the teacher.

Case recap

At Sargh, Umar hears of plague in al-Sham and consults groups who disagree about proceeding. Some stress the journey’s purpose; others stress those accompanying him. He decides to return. Abu Ubayda raises God’s decree; Umar explains that prudent choices can coexist with it. Abd al-Rahman then reports Muhammad’s instruction against entering an affected land or fleeing one where already present. The student’s local work changes messages, trust, supplies and responsibilities; it does not determine Umar’s decision.

Essential terms: caliph: leader of the Muslim community; al-Sham: broader Syrian region; decree: what God ordains; precaution: action intended to reduce danger; consultation: seeking others’ advice. These are episode concepts, not a verified weekly vocabulary list.

Reading connection: Bukhari 5729 and Muslim 2219a. The grazing analogy presents practical choice within faith. These are later transmitted accounts, not a contemporary transcript. Michael Dols’s historical discussion supports the approximate 638–639 setting. Chronology and later interpretations vary.

Teacher provenance: Rashid, Nadir, Harith, their work and dialogue are authored. Solidarity arguments are not all attributed to Abu Ubayda. Leaders are narrated without invented quotations or portraits. The decision precedes the newly reported instruction. No branch guarantees aid delivery, health, or news of Harith. This is a historical ethical case, not current medical guidance.

Principles Portfolio — exactly three short answers

  1. What should the character do? Why?
    “They should ___ because ___.” Use one episode detail. Choose Umar, Rashid or Nadir and make clear whom you mean.
  2. Why might someone choose differently?
    “Someone might choose ___ because ___.” Give a reason worth taking seriously.
  3. Choose ONE:
    • How can Rashid and Nadir share faith in God yet disagree about continuing? Use one reason from each side.
    • What if the travelling party could provide help that nobody else could offer? Would your recommendation change? Why?
    • When does turning back become abandonment? Explain what responsibility should remain after Umar’s decision.

One or two short sentences each is enough. The “What if” is a hypothetical change, not a historical fact. Support starters: “A leader should accept risk when ___”; “Protecting a group still requires ___.” These are optional beginnings, not model answers. Students may defend or revise their judgement. Writing is separate from gameplay and does not gate it.

Knowledge check

Four questions, 25 app points each; three correct permits completion with review/retry. Questions cover the outbreak report, fair representation of both positions, Umar’s analogy, and the instruction’s distinction between entering and fleeing. No opinion earns points. App points do not automatically become Canvas grades.

Optional fortnightly presentation

Solo or pairs, equal status. Four slides; about 4–6 minutes including an audience choice. Put reasoning in speaker notes, around 200–300 words across the deck as guidance, not a quota.

  1. Case and audience choice: place, immediate dilemma, source.
  2. Principle and reasons: one specific detail, strongest opposing reason, and how shared belief can support differing judgements.
  3. Hypothetical counterexample that tests the same principle: change useful help, consent, or information rather than inventing a different historical outcome.
  4. Revised or defended principle, remaining limitation, audience objection and response.

Each student adds a labelled 40–60-word individual note on slide 4 explaining their judgement, reconsideration or defence, and contribution. Submit notes with slides; a slides-only PDF is insufficient. This companion does not create a new presentation assignment.

Teacher-only assessment cues

Weekly reflection: completion with revision for relevant answers and an episode detail. Do not grade faith, disagreement, writing length or English polish. For a requested presentation, use five 0–2 criteria: accurate case plus 1b.1; supported judgement; fair alternative; counterexample and response; individual reasoning. Assess the artifact, not presumed delivery.

Two defensible paths: prioritise travellers’ exposure while retaining concrete duties to those ahead; or prioritise the value of presence while specifying useful tasks, limits and who may accept risk. A strong student can also defend a bounded pause while acknowledging its costs. None requires claiming an outcome proves the choice correct.

Likely misconception: “One side has faith and the other uses reason.” Ask: “Which shared belief do both accept, and where do their views of responsibility diverge?” Also challenge the claims that keeping supplies is free of cost, or reserving them means aid has arrived.

Feedback stem: “You explain ___ clearly. Strengthen your comparison by showing why someone sharing that belief might still prioritise ___.” Advanced extension: expose an assumption about useful help or consent; do not simply write more.

Suitability: understandable competing duties, shared factual recap on every route, six meaningful choices, no morality score, no required belief change. New episode only; earlier published packages and saved answers preserved.
