The Price of Peace

Case recap

Egypt and Hatti made a peace treaty around 1259 BCE under Ramesses II and Hattusili III. Alongside peace and mutual obligations, its provisions addressed returning fugitives and protecting returned people. In our invented case, Aruna fears local harm despite that promise. An Egyptian official must weigh return with recorded protections, a short delay to seek a responsible receiving official, or refusal with higher review. Each has costs. The story does not establish whether Aruna’s feared harm would actually occur.

Historical boundary: The treaty is real. Aruna, Dedu, Tili, their dialogue, this individual case, local review arrangements, deadlines, messages and branch outcomes are invented. The player does not change the historical treaty. The two changed-evidence scenarios are hypotheticals.

Reading connection: Who gets to decide what is right? distinguishes authority, custom, reasons and consequences, and written ideals from lived practice. The Egyptian concept of ma’at connects truth, justice and right order; invoking it does not remove the need to give reasons. This is a paraphrase and application, not an ancient quotation.

Essential terms

Treaty: agreement between political powers. Fugitive: a person who has fled; the label alone does not establish guilt. Protection: safeguards against harm. Implementation: putting a promise into practice. Accountability: arrangements for answering questions and correcting failures. Evidence: information that supports or challenges a claim. These are episode concepts, not a replacement for the weekly Socrates vocabulary list.

Your Decision Note

Write about 80–120 words total, or five concise bullets. This is separate from gameplay and never prevents finishing the episode. Keep your original response for later reconsideration.

  1. Choose: What should Dedu do about Aruna’s return?
  2. Explain and compare — 3b.3: Give your strongest reason and a specific episode detail. Compare one benefit and one cost of your response with those of another response; say who bears the cost.
  3. Explain the other side: What is the strongest reasonable argument for that alternative?
  4. State a principle: What general rule supports your judgement?
  5. Test it: What changed fact might make you reconsider that rule? Explain why it matters. You may retain the rule if you can defend it against the challenge.

Frames: “The need is…” · “This detail matters because…” · “My response protects…, but costs…; the alternative…” · “Someone could reasonably disagree because…” · “My rule becomes difficult when…”

Optional principle starters: “A leader should keep an agreement when…” · “A promise to protect someone should be checked by…” · “A temporary delay is justified if…” Complete your own rule; these are openings, not answers.

Optional test: Suppose reliable independent witnesses report that previous returnees were protected—or that they were harmed. Explain which fact matters to your rule. For a greater challenge, expose an assumption or design a different counterexample; do not simply write more.

Assessment: Completion with revision: relevant attempts at all five prompts and a specific detail. Neither agreement with the teacher nor polished English is required. The four in-game knowledge MCQs are separate: 25 app points each, three correct to finish, with review and retry. App points do not automatically become Canvas grades.

Teacher-only marking cues

Observable focus evidence: Prompt 2 and slide 2 notes identify the need, compare two real options, and explain benefits/costs for affected people. Do not claim that a simulation demonstrates implemented leadership or service.

Two defensible reasoning paths: A student may support return because reciprocal commitments protect many people, while acknowledging uncertain enforcement and specifying follow-up. Another may support a bounded delay because a concrete information gap can be investigated, while acknowledging separation, administrative costs and diplomatic trust. Refusal can also be defended with a proportionate account of risk and its costs. None is a model answer to give students before submission.

Likely misconception: “The treaty guarantees safety, so evidence is unnecessary”—or its opposite, “uncertainty proves harm.” Distinguish a commitment, a report and independent confirmation. Do not present the ancient agreement as a modern universal refugee-rights regime.

Focus feedback: “You explained what your choice protects. Who bears its cost, and why is that cost justified compared with the alternative?” Give one strength and one next step. Use whole-class feedback for shared difficulties.
