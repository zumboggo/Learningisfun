The Institution Protects Its Own

Kevin discovers that a charity director changed a scholarship score to help a longtime volunteer’s daughter. Her fear of losing donors competes with two applicants’ immediate needs. Six choices explore correction, independent review, disclosure, privacy and loyalty. About 12–15 minutes plus the knowledge check; allow more time for multilingual readers and discussion.

Primary standard 4b.1: Connect a conclusion to reasons and show how they support it.
Student objective: Support your proposed response with a verified case detail and explain why it matters.
Success: Separate evidence from prediction, use a specific detail, and address a serious opposing reason. No preferred reporting route is graded.

Case recap and essential terms

Mara raises Aya’s total from 78 to 86 outside the rules, placing her above Rafi’s 84. Aya did not request this. Rafi needs a decision before another offer expires. The sponsor has not decided whether to renew. All routes restore the original ranking, but the review, publicity and safeguards differ. Aya still needs support; an award is not guaranteed to her.

Institution: an organized body with roles and rules. Conflict of interest: a competing attachment that may interfere with a responsibility. Accountability: having to explain decisions and face checks or consequences. Impartiality: applying relevant standards without favoring insiders. Prediction: a claim about what may happen, not proof that it has happened.

Pair with Your Dad Stole a Sheep. Now What? and The Sheep Behind Our House. Henrich’s historical account concerns cooperation beyond kin; this case tests whether institutions can still favor insiders. It cannot prove or disprove his historical explanation.

Three short answers in the Principles Portfolio

  1. What should the character do? Why? “Kevin should ___ because ___.” Use one case detail.
  2. Why might someone choose differently? “Someone might choose ___ because ___.” Explain a reason worth taking seriously.
  3. Choose one dropdown question:
    • What fact best supports your chosen response to Mara, and why does it matter?
    • What if the sponsor definitely would withdraw next year’s funding? Would your response change? Why?
    • Can Kevin be loyal to the charity while refusing to volunteer there? Why?

One or two short sentences each are enough. Writing remains separate from gameplay and never gates it. Four knowledge questions award 25 points each; three correct allows completion, with review/retry. Narrative choices earn no points.

Debate preparation

Motion: Loyalty to an institution sometimes requires exposing its wrongdoing. Define “exposing”: telling an internal reviewer, affected people, or everybody? Compare your definition with your opponent’s before arguing.

Use “My conclusion is ___; the fact ___ supports it because ___.” Then try “The strongest objection is ___; my response is ___.” Test one changed detail: the sponsor has definitely threatened withdrawal, or the board refuses to keep the original records. Students may defend their original principle if it survives the challenge. No disclosure of personal wrongdoing is required.

Optional later presentation: solo or pairs

Equal status and the same four-slide task. (1) Case and audience choice. (2) Principle and reasons: connect one verified fact to the conclusion and represent the strongest alternative. (3) A changed detail that tests the same principle. (4) A revised principle or defended original, one limitation, and an audience objection with a response.

Keep reasoning in speaker notes, about 200–300 words as guidance rather than a quota. Each student adds a labelled 40–60-word individual note to slide 4 explaining a reason they endorse, what they reconsidered or defended, and their contribution. Allow 4–6 minutes in small presentation circles. This guide does not create an additional assignment.

Teacher-only review

Assess the artifact, not unobserved performance, accent or polish. Five criteria, each 0–2: accurate case plus standards focus; supported judgement; fair alternative; genuine counterexample with reasoned revision or retention; individual reasoning. Zero = missing/mistaken, one = partial, two = clear and supported. For partners, the first four may share a score; the fifth is individual.

Two plausible reasoning paths: A documented internal correction can prioritize Rafi’s urgent deadline while retaining oversight and appeal; it must face the problem of insiders reviewing insiders. Independent review or applicant disclosure can reduce dependence on Mara’s assurances; it must face cost, delay, privacy and possible undeserved blame. A good answer names what its preferred route leaves unresolved.

Likely misconception: a good mission excuses a bad method; an outside reviewer guarantees fairness; or feared donor loss is an established consequence. Focus feedback: Which fact supports your conclusion, and what connecting reason makes it relevant? Success cue: The student can distinguish “the sponsor may leave” from “the sponsor has decided to leave.”

Source and portrayal boundary

This is a wholly invented contemporary charity case with authored procedures and dialogue, not an allegation about a real institution or legal guidance. The pre-story notice discloses this. Sources and provenance remain outside the narrative. The episode deliberately protects Aya’s agency and keeps her family circumstances out of broad disclosure.
