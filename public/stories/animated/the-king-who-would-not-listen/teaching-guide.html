ETHICS AND LEADERSHIP · THURSDAY, OCTOBER 22, 2026

The King Who Would Not Listen

Can wisdom justify giving one person the final say?

12–15 minutes of story, four knowledge questions, then 8–10 minutes for the separate portfolio. Seven decisions; no moral score. Three correct knowledge answers permit completion.

Focus and success

2a.1 — Compare belief and action. Student objective: explain whether a character’s specific action fits a belief they claim to hold. Observable evidence: the portfolio’s reason and episode detail, developed in the presentation notes. Success: accurately name a belief, identify an action, explain the fit or tension, and acknowledge a reasonable alternative interpretation. Agreement with the teacher is not the criterion.

The case

Plato visits Syracuse hoping, with Dion, to educate Dionysius II. A powerful ruler can order useful changes quickly, but can also reject criticism without surrendering authority. Dion is exiled; the philosophical reform does not create an ideal government. Plato later returns, unsuccessfully. Dion subsequently overthrows Dionysius and is eventually assassinated. Changing the person does not settle how power should be controlled.

The player helps fictional copyist Maia deliver a harbour complaint and draft advice. A possible inquiry, a grain rumour and restrictions on speech expose competing needs: safety, expertise, public participation, truth and accountability. These incidents and proposals are invented. Students do not enact a historical constitution or prevent Dion’s exile.

Essential terms: Philosopher-king: Plato’s ideal joining philosophical understanding and rule. Episteme / doxa: knowledge / opinion; certainty alone does not establish knowledge. Censorship: restricting expression or access to it. Accountability: having to answer for decisions and face meaningful challenge. Democracy: political power exercised by citizens; ancient citizenship excluded many residents. Rule of law: authority constrained by laws rather than personal preference; enforcement still matters.

Five portfolio questions

Use the episode’s Principles Portfolio. Write five concise responses, roughly 80–120 words altogether as guidance. Submit hides the form while preserving answers under See all answers. No duplicate Canvas writing is required.

  1. Choose: What should the person do? Why?
  2. Explain: Give your strongest reason and one specific detail from the episode.
  3. Consider another view: What is the strongest reason for a different choice?
  4. State your principle: What general principle would help you or others in this situation? How might you apply the principle in one other situation?
  5. Test your principle: What change to the situation might make you reconsider it? Explain why.

Focus cue for question 2: Compare one action with a stated belief: the king welcomes advice but exiles Dion; Plato challenges the ruler while supporting restrictions on some educational stories. Explain the tension rather than merely calling someone a hypocrite.

Optional frames: “This action fits/conflicts with the belief because…”; “Someone could reasonably disagree because…”; “A leader should be allowed to… only when…”; “My principle becomes difficult when…”

Possible tests: The next ruler refuses education; the king appoints every appeal judge; a majority wants to silence a minority; an immediate threat makes a delay dangerous. Choose a change relevant to your own principle. Advanced challenge: identify its hidden assumption or construct a stronger counterexample. Retaining a defensible principle is as valuable as revising it.

Presentation handoff

Use this or the previous episode at the next scheduled presentation checkpoint. This guide does not create an extra assessment. Work solo or in pairs with equal expectations, in presentation circles of about four students; 4–6 minutes including an audience choice.

  1. Story and choice: concise dilemma and audience choice; notes explain context, source and accurate details.
  2. Principle and reasons: principle plus a few cue words; notes connect a specific action to a stated belief, develop your reason and the strongest opposing reason.
  3. Counterexample: a labelled hypothetical change; notes explain why it tests the same principle.
  4. Revised or defended principle: notes justify your response, identify a remaining limitation, and record one audience objection with your reply.

Keep slides visual and concise; put assessable reasoning in notes (about 200–300 words as guidance). Each student adds a labelled 40–60-word individual note on slide 4 explaining their reasoning, what they reconsidered or defended, and their contribution. Submit the deck with notes or an accessible equivalent including notes. Do not grade decorative polish or presume unseen speaking performance.

Teacher-only marking and discussion cues

Weekly portfolio: completion with revision for relevant attempts, an episode detail and reasons. Ask “Which action, which stated belief, and why does the relation matter?” Avoid rewarding a particular political conclusion.

Two defensible reasoning paths: conditional engagement may secure a concrete benefit while resisting public endorsement; withdrawal may prevent a philosopher’s reputation from legitimising unchecked authority. Each must face its own costs. Neither is guaranteed to improve the historical outcome.

Likely misconceptions: Plato is a modern free-speech democrat; all expertise is tyranny; all majority votes are just; laws enforce themselves; a successful inquiry proves unlimited rule is good; the fictional harbour case is documented history. Dion’s removal also involves a real struggle for influence, so do not reduce the court to a simple honest-teacher/bad-pupil story.

Presentation rubric, 10 points, five criteria at 0–2: accurate case plus 2a.1 application; supported judgment; fair alternative; genuine counterexample and justified revision/retention; individual reasoning. 0 absent/mistaken, 1 partial, 2 clear and supported. First four may share pair scores; the fifth is individual. Provide one strength and one next step.

Sources and boundaries

  • Plutarch, Life of Dion, especially 9–20; later biographical events appear later in the Life. Written centuries after the events, with moral and literary shaping.
  • Seventh Letter, especially 327–337 and 339–350. Its authorship is disputed. Scholarly discussion of Plato at Syracuse.
  • Plato, Republic II–III, V 473c–e, VI and VIII. Stanford overview of its ethics and politics. All episode speech is invented paraphrase, not authentic quotation.
