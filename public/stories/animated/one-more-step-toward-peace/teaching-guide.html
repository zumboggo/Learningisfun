One More Step Toward Peace

Ethics and Leadership · October 23, 2026
10–12 minute episode, knowledge check, then a separate 8–10 minute Decision Note.

The case

In 1965, defending South Vietnam and preserving American credibility make escalation seem attractive to McNamara, despite a warning of a larger stalemate. By June 1967 he recommends limits. In Quảng Ngãi, Trâm and her colleagues prepare for the next patient. You can raise objections and propose alternatives in the office; none can guarantee peace. The brief 1968 thread documents campaign contacts discouraging South Vietnamese participation in talks without claiming that peace was otherwise certain.

Essential terms: proxy support—outside powers support local forces; direct intervention—their own forces fight; escalation—an increase in force or involvement; leverage—an advantage in bargaining; counterfactual—a proposed alternative course whose outcome remains uncertain.

Reading connection: Kant asks us to treat people as ends with purposes of their own, never merely as instruments. This is a paraphrase of the second formulation in Groundwork, chapter 2. Consider whether promising benefits is enough to respect those who bear the risks.

Decision Note · five concise bullets or 80–120 words

  1. Choose: What should McNamara recommend: escalation with a stopping test, a bombing pause and talks, or a ceiling on involvement?
  2. Explain and compare: Identify the need your proposal addresses. Compare its likely benefit and cost with one alternative, using a specific episode detail. Where is the outcome uncertain?
  3. Explain the other side: What is the strongest reasonable objection to your proposal?
  4. State a principle: What general rule supports your judgement?
  5. Test it: What meaningful change might make you reconsider your rule—or defend it more carefully?

Frames: “This detail matters because…” “Compared with…, my proposal risks…” “Someone could reasonably disagree because…” “My rule becomes difficult when…”

Optional starters: protect allies while limiting foreseeable harm; do not increase force without evidence and a stopping condition; include affected people’s purposes in decisions. Test a rule by supposing an ally faces immediate collapse, a pause brings no response, or credible evidence shows escalation will worsen civilian danger. Advanced challenge: expose an assumption behind both proposals. Keep your original note for later reconsideration.

Presentation handoff

Four slides: (1) story and choice; (2) principle, reasons and a comparison of costs/benefits; (3) a clearly hypothetical change that tests the same principle; (4) a revised principle or a defended original, with one limitation. Teacher notes

Primary standard 3b.3: identify a need and compare two responses using relevant benefits and costs. Objective: compare two defensible responses to an ally’s danger, including risks borne by Vietnamese people. Success: identifies the need, explains a benefit and cost or uncertainty for each response, and uses a specific detail.

Story choices have no moral score. Four knowledge questions award 25 app points each; 75 permits completion, with explanations and retry. The Decision Note is separate, formative completion with revision: relevant attempts at all five prompts plus a specific detail. No writing gates gameplay.

Two defensible reasoning paths: a limited commitment may reduce exposure while abandoning people who relied on promises; conditional escalation may protect an ally while worsening a stalemate. Neither earns credit solely for its conclusion. Misconception: a pause guarantees peace, or local allies and opponents simply obey foreign sponsors. Feedback: “Whose need and whose cost did your comparison leave out?”

For later presentations, five criteria at 0–2 each: accurate case plus 3b.3 comparison; supported judgement; strongest alternative; genuine counterexample and justified response; individual reasoning. 0 absent/mistaken, 1 partial, 2 clear and supported. First four may share a pair score; the last is individual. Assess artifact evidence, not assumed oral confidence.

Suitability: competing demands are protection, credibility, local agency and human harm. Usable evidence includes the 1965 larger-stalemate warning, the 1967 limits, and Trâm’s hospital/diary perspective.
