Courage Without Applause

Six decisions about safety, trust and honour. A rescue score out of 100, with public reputation shown separately.

Play the episode · Read Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II

Teaching focus

1b.1: Explain and evaluate why a person holding a studied worldview might choose differently.

Student objective: Explain how Aristotle's ideas about habit, purpose and appropriate judgement could guide a decision differently from seeking applause, then evaluate both reasons.

Evidence and success: In the Decision Note, accurately connect one choice to Aristotle, use a particular episode detail, and fairly explain a competing reason. Agreement with a preferred rescue method is not required.

Allow approximately 10–12 minutes for the story, four knowledge questions, then 8–10 minutes for the separate Decision Note. Reading is not timed. Each of six major decisions advances one turn.

Case recap and reading

Melas waits across a damaged crossing. His son Nikias wants to help and recover public respect. Doria wants both men safe; Bion has expertise but speaks harshly. The player coordinates the team from firm ground. Preparations, cooperation and reputation affect the feasible rescue routes. Every route resolves Melas's return, but delays and injuries differ.

Book II.1: repeated actions form habits. II.2 and II.6–9: the appropriate response depends on the occasion; the mean is not an arithmetic middle. II.4: acting virtuously involves knowledge, choosing the act for its own sake, and a stable disposition. A favourable outcome or one impressive act cannot establish virtue.

Essential terms: habit—an acquired pattern of action; virtue—a good disposition of character; rashness—excessive confidence about danger; mean—the appropriate response relative to the person and situation; honour—public esteem.

Source boundary: The rescue, supporting people and dialogue are authored. The opening line “What kind of person are you practising becoming?” is inspired by Aristotle, not an authentic quotation. The historical setting is broad fourth-century-BCE context. The rescue mechanics are a classroom game, not practical rescue instructions.

Decision Note

Write about 80–120 words total, or five concise bullets. Use the episode notebook if assigned in the app; writing is separate from gameplay. The four-question knowledge check assesses understanding, not agreement.

  1. Choose: What should the team do at one difficult decision?
  2. Explain: Give your strongest reason and one specific episode detail.
  3. Explain the other side: How might someone guided by Aristotle choose differently from someone mainly seeking public honour? Explain the strongest reason for the alternative and evaluate it.
  4. State a principle: What general rule supports your judgement?
  5. Test it: What change to the situation might make you reconsider that rule?

Frames: “This detail matters because…” “Aristotle might ask whether…” “Someone could reasonably disagree because…” “My rule becomes difficult when…”

Optional principle starters: “Protect someone's dignity when…” “Accept a risk to help when…” “Public respect should matter when…” Choose one; complete it yourself.

Counterexample cue: Suppose losing public respect means nobody will lend the equipment tomorrow. Does your principle survive? You may defend your original rule if you explain why. For an additional challenge, identify an assumption in the game's scoring.

Completion with revision: attempt all five prompts and use a specific detail. Do not grade polished English, length, score maximisation or a preferred moral conclusion.

Presentation development

For the next scheduled presentation, work solo or in a pair with the same task and workload. Develop this Decision Note or the other eligible week's note. Use four slides; put the reasoning in speaker notes, approximately 200–300 words as guidance.

  1. Story and choice: Show a vivid detail and invite the audience to choose. Notes explain the case and source.
  2. Principle and reasons: Show your rule. Notes explain a detail, Aristotle's relevant view, your evaluation and the strongest opposing reason.
  3. Counterexample: Change one morally relevant feature. Notes explain how it challenges the same principle.
  4. Revised or defended principle: Explain how your rule handles both cases and one remaining limit. After presenting, add an audience objection and your response.

Each student adds a labelled 40–60-word individual note to slide 4 explaining a personally endorsed reason, what they reconsidered or defended, and their contribution. Submit the deck with notes or an accessible equivalent with a notes export. Suggested presentation circles: about four students, 4–6 minutes per presentation including an audience choice.

Teacher-only marking cues

Five criteria, 0–2 each: accurate case and 1b.1 application; supported judgement; fair alternative; genuine counterexample and response; individual reasoning. Grade the submitted slides and notes, not unseen oral delivery.

Plausible reasoning paths: A student can defend preserving dignity because it sustains cooperation, while distinguishing this from flattering a dangerous plan. Another can defend a public correction because a shared mistake threatens everyone, while acknowledging the cost to trust and repairing it.

Likely misconception: “Aristotle always chooses the middle option.” Ask which feature of this case makes the response appropriate, and whether its position on the menu matters.

Focus feedback: “You name Aristotle's idea. Explain how it changes the decision compared with the strongest honour-based reason.”

Scoring critique: Rescue 50 + harm prevention 30 + cooperation 20. Reputation remains separate. These are explicit authored priorities, not measurements of moral worth. All outcomes are deterministic and reconstruct from legal choices; a replay can compare different priorities.
